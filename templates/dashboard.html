Deception+

Pitcher results beyond what the pitch's measured traits predict.

A model predicts how hitters should do against each pitch from its speed, spin, movement and location, plus the batter, the catcher, the count and the park. Deception+ measures how far a pitcher's actual results land above or below those predictions.

The short version

What it is

For every pitch, a model predicts whether the hitter whiffs, chases or makes weak contact, and how well the swing is timed. Deception+ adds up how far a pitcher's actual results beat those predictions. {{N_COMPOSITE}} gaps go in (whiff, chase, weak contact, swing timing and how far the bat misses by), and the steadier gaps count for more.

How to read a number

100 is the average qualified pitcher and every 10 points is one step, so 120 is two steps above average. Every score comes with a 95% interval, shown on the leaderboard. Check it before ranking two close pitchers.

What it does not prove

The score is whatever the model cannot explain, so calling it deception is a hypothesis. It correlates {{STUFF_R}} with Stuff+. Sequencing, tunneling, spin mirroring, tempo and arm slot were each tested as explanations and none accounts for it (cross-validated R² {{DRIVER_R2_RANGE}} at the season level, {{SEQ_R2_RANGE}} at the pitch level).

Every prediction is made for pitchers the model never trained on, so a pitcher's own pitches never help predict their own results. Does it just track Stuff+?

Partly. The two correlate at r={{STUFF_R}} (95% interval {{STUFF_R_CI}}), so pitchers with better stuff tend to score higher, but Stuff+ accounts for only a small part of the score.

Among {{STUFF_N}} qualified pitcher-seasons with a Stuff+ figure the correlation is {{STUFF_R_BY_SEASON}} by season, and for each piece of the score it is {{STUFF_R_MEMBERS}}. The model controls for each pitch's own measured velocity, spin, movement, release and location, but Stuff+ contains information beyond those measurements, so the score still tracks it. Stuff+ comes from FanGraphs, was entered by hand, and the model never uses it.

How to read this table

Steady within a season is a split-half correlation: odd games against even games, Spearman-Brown corrected. Matches next season is the year-over-year correlation. The forecast columns test whether a pitcher's score in one season predicts their rate the next season after accounting for what that next season's own expectation already predicts, with every pair of consecutive seasons stacked. ΔR² comes from 5-fold cross-validation grouped by pitcher, and p from a test of the added term with standard errors clustered on pitcher. A p below 0.01 counts as a pass.

Before you quote a score

  • Stability depends on how much a pitcher threw. Among pairs of consecutive seasons in which the pitcher qualified in both, the year-over-year correlation is {{YOY_QUAL_R}}. The headline {{YOY_R}} includes small samples.
  • Qualified relievers average {{RP_MEAN}} against {{SP_MEAN}} for starters, even with in-game fatigue and times through the order in the model, so part of the gap may be role. The leaderboard can filter by starter or reliever.
  • Left-handed pitchers average {{LHP_GAP}} points against right-handed ones ({{LHP_N}} against {{RHP_N}} qualified pitcher-seasons, p={{LHP_P}}). Handedness is a model input, so the gap is either real or a leftover miscalibration, and these data cannot tell which.
  • Three completed regular seasons (2024 to 2026), so there are no aging curves and only two year-over-year pairs.
