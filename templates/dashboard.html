Deception+

Deception+ measures how far a pitcher's results land above or below what a model predicts from each pitch's speed, spin, movement and location, plus the batter, catcher, count and park.

It does not prove deception. It is the part of results the model cannot explain.

How a score is built

A score is a pitcher's results minus what a model predicted, set so 100 is average and 10 points is one step.

  1. Predict

    A model rates every pitch from its speed, spin, movement, location, the batter, catcher, count and park.

    {{EX_N}} swings

  2. Compare

    Actual results minus the prediction, averaged over the pitcher's pitches.

    +{{EX_RAW}} points

  3. Shrink

    Small samples are pulled toward average, so luck does not look like skill.

    +{{EX_SHRUNK}} points

  4. Scale

    Counted in standard deviations above average, then set to 100 plus 10 per step.

    {{EX_Z}} steps, so {{EX_INDEX}}

  5. Blend

    {{N_COMPOSITE}} areas (whiff, chase, weak contact, timing, whiff miss distance) are averaged, steadier ones weighted more.

    {{TOP1_SCORE}}

Worked example: {{TOP1_NAME}}, {{TOP1_SEASON}}, whiff area. Points are percentage points of whiff rate per swing.

How to read a number

100 is average and 10 points is one step. Every score has a 95% interval on the leaderboard. Check it before ranking two close pitchers.

What it does not prove

It is whatever the model cannot explain, so calling it deception is a hypothesis. It tracks Stuff+ (r={{STUFF_R}}), and sequencing, tunneling, spin mirroring, tempo and arm slot each explain at most {{DRIVER_R2_MAX_PCT}} of it.

Can you trust a score?

Partly. Scores carry over from one season to the next, but each has a wide margin of error.

Is it just Stuff+?

Partly. Better stuff tends to score higher, but Stuff+ explains only about {{STUFF_R2_PCT}} of the differences in Deception+.

The model already controls for each pitch's measured traits. The overlap may be information Stuff+ has that the model lacks. Adding fastball gaps to the model lowered it but also lowered the score's stability, so they were not adopted.

Reading a score

A pitcher's results minus what the model predicted for their pitches, scaled so 100 is average. Above 100 means results beat the prediction.

Where a score comes from

Expected value
What the model predicts for a pitch from its speed, spin, movement and location, plus the batter, catcher, count and park. What the pitcher gets beyond that is the score.
Gradient-boosted models by pitch type, averaged over several random splits of pitchers.
Shrinkage
Pulling a small-sample result toward average. The fewer the pitches, the stronger the pull, so a hot month is not mistaken for skill.
Here {{TOP1_NAME}}'s whiff piece starts at +{{EX_RAW}} points over {{EX_N}} swings and ends at +{{EX_SHRUNK}}.
Empirical Bayes. The spread between pitchers is estimated with the Paule-Mandel method.
Whiff
Swings that miss, as a share of swings.
Chase
Swings at pitches outside the zone, as a share of those pitches.
Weak contact
Balls in play hit under 85 mph, as a share of balls in play.
Timing
How far from the hitter's ideal contact depth the bat meets the ball, early or late, on swings that connect.
Whiff miss distance
On a swing and miss, how far the ball passed the barrel. A bigger miss scores higher.
Log scale, from Statcast bat tracking.
Blend
A weighted average of the pieces a pitcher has data for. Steadier pieces and bigger samples count more.
Each weight is the piece's league-wide split-half reliability times the pitcher's sample size, relative to the median.
Left out of the score

How much to trust it

r (correlation)
How closely two numbers move together. 0 is no link, 1 is a perfect match, and a minus sign means one falls as the other rises. Squaring r gives the share of differences one number explains.
Here Deception+ and Stuff+ have r={{STUFF_R}}, so Stuff+ explains about {{STUFF_R2_PCT}}.
Pearson r. This page's wording: {{R_GUIDE}}. Interval for the Stuff+ r: {{STUFF_R_CI}}.
Reliability
Score odd and even games separately, then compare the two sets. High means the score reflects something repeatable. Low means luck.
Here Deception+ overall is {{COMPOSITE_R}}. The steadiest piece is {{MOST_LABEL}} ({{MOST_R}}) and the noisiest is {{LEAST_LABEL}} ({{LEAST_R}}).
Split-half r with the Spearman-Brown correction, which projects half a season up to a full one.
Year-over-year r
The same pitchers' scores in one season against the next. It runs lower than reliability because skill, role and health also change.
Here {{YOY_R}} over {{YOY_N}} season pairs, and {{YOY_QUAL_R}} over the {{YOY_QUAL_N}} pairs where the pitcher qualified both years.
Consecutive seasons pooled. Interval for the qualified pairs: {{YOY_QUAL_LO}} to {{YOY_QUAL_HI}}, resampling pitchers.
95% interval
A range built so that 95 of every 100 like it hold the true value. If it clears zero (a gain) or 100 (a score), the result is clear. If it covers that mark, the data cannot separate the two.
Here The typical score is good to ±{{DP_HALF}} points, so {{DP_CLEAR}} of {{N_QUALIFIED}} qualified scores ({{DP_CLEAR_PCT}}) clearly differ from 100.
Scores use the wider of a game bootstrap and an analytic interval. The leaderboard marks a score whose interval covers 100 with a hollow circle.
Bootstrap
Re-score a pitcher many times, each time on a random redraw of their games, and see how far the answer moves.
Resamples whole games with replacement, because pitches within a game are not independent.
Design effect
Pitches in one game share conditions, so they tell you less than the same number of independent pitches. Intervals are widened to match.
Here It runs {{DE_RANGE}} across outcomes, and 1.00 means no widening.
A variance multiplier estimated from the game bootstrap. Starters carry more of it than relievers.
Interval check
A test of whether the intervals are honest. Build the score and interval from half of a pitcher's games, then compare them with the other half's result.
Here The standardized misses have a standard deviation of {{COV_SD_RANGE}} (1 is exact), and {{COV_95_RANGE}} fall inside the 95% line.
It tests noise within a season, not the distance between a score and a pitcher's lasting skill.

Whether the models are sound

Out of fold
Predictions from a model that never saw that pitcher. Pitchers are split into groups, the model trains on all but one group and predicts the one left out, and each group takes a turn.
Folds are grouped by pitcher, so a pitcher's own pitches never help predict their own results.
AUC
Take one pitch that drew a whiff and one that did not. AUC is how often the model rated the whiff higher: 0.5 is a coin flip and 1.0 is perfect.
Here Whiff {{VAL_AUC_WHIFF}} and chase {{VAL_AUC_CHASE}}, across pitch types.
Area under the ROC curve, scored out of fold.
Calibration slope
Whether a stated chance means what it says. When the model says 30%, does it happen about 30% of the time? A slope of 1.0 means yes, and a little above 1.0 means predictions sit slightly too close to average.
Here The scored pieces run {{VAL_SLOPE_MEMBERS}}.
Slope of actual on predicted, out of fold.

Predicting results

R²
The share of differences between pitchers that a model explains. 0.23 means 23%. It is scored on pitchers the model did not train on, so memorizing cannot inflate it.
Here In the first row of the on-field table, {{OV_EXAMPLE}}.
Cross-validated, with folds grouped by pitcher.
Forecast gain (ΔR²)
Next season's actual rate, such as whiff rate, is first predicted from what that season's pitches would predict. The gain is the extra share explained once this season's score for the piece is added. Zero means it added nothing.
Here Timing's gain is {{DELTA_TIMING}} of R².
Change in cross-validated R², with errors clustered on pitcher.
Beyond Stuff+ and Location+
The on-field test first fits a model with those two ratings, then adds Deception+. A gain is information the ratings lack. The stricter column also holds last season's own result for the same stat.
Both models are scored on held-out pitchers.
p-value (p)
The chance of a result this large if the piece truly added nothing. Smaller means it is harder to blame on luck. It does not say how big the effect is.
Here A piece passes the forecast test when p is below {{P_GATE}}.
Errors clustered on pitcher.
Clustered on pitcher
One pitcher's seasons resemble each other, so they are not independent rows. Intervals and p-values count each pitcher as one unit, which keeps them from looking more certain than they are.
Cluster-robust standard errors. Correlation intervals resample pitchers.
Season pair
One pitcher's season and the season after it. Forecasts and on-field gains are measured on these.
Here {{OV_PAIRS}} pairs from {{OV_PITCHERS}} pitchers, each with at least {{OV_MIN_PA}} batters faced in the second season.
Stuff+ and Location+
FanGraphs ratings of a pitcher's pitch quality (Stuff+, from the pitch's physical traits) and placement (Location+). 100 is average.
Entered by hand. Stuff+ is never a model input.
xwOBA and wOBA allowed
Weighted on-base average counts each way of reaching base by what it is worth. xwOBA swaps what happened on balls in play for what that exit speed and launch angle usually produce, which strips out some luck and defense. Lower is better for a pitcher.
xwOBA keeps the actual values for strikeouts and walks.
Strikeout rate and walk rate
The share of batters faced who strike out or walk.
Run value per 100 pitches
Statcast's estimate of how many runs each pitch result changed the game by, totaled per 100 pitches.
Pitcher run expectancy change.

Before you quote a score

  • Volume matters: year-to-year r is {{YOY_QUAL_R}} when the pitcher qualified both seasons, {{YOY_R}} overall.
  • Relievers average {{RP_MEAN}} against {{SP_MEAN}} for starters even after adjusting for fatigue, so part of the gap may be role.
  • Left-handers average {{LHP_GAP}} points against right-handers (p={{LHP_P}}). The gap is real or leftover model error, and these data cannot say which.
  • {{N_SEASONS}} seasons means no aging curves and only {{N_SEASON_PAIRS}} season-to-season pairs.
